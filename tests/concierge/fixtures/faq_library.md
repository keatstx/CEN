# Use Case 1: Charity Care

---

### Stage: Awareness

**Q1: What exactly is charity care, and why should families in our community know about it?**
**A (Short):** Charity care is free or reduced-cost hospital care that Illinois law requires nonprofit hospitals to offer qualifying low-income patients — and most families who qualify are never told about it.
**A (Full):** Under Illinois law, nonprofit hospitals must provide free care to patients whose household income is at or below 200% of the Federal Poverty Level (FPL) — roughly $62,400 per year for a family of four in 2025. Patients with incomes above that threshold may still qualify for discounted care on a sliding scale. The problem is that hospitals are not required to proactively tell patients they qualify, so most families leave with bills they could have had forgiven. FairClaims and the CEN team exist specifically to close that gap — we find the families, screen them, and make sure they receive every dollar of relief they're entitled to.

**Q2: How do I explain the 200% FPL income threshold to a family during an outreach conversation?**
**A (Short):** Tell them that in 2025, a family of four earning up to about $62,400 a year likely qualifies for completely free hospital care under Illinois law.
**A (Full):** The Federal Poverty Level is a federal benchmark that changes slightly each year; 200% of that level for a family of four comes to approximately $62,400 in 2025. A single person qualifies at roughly $30,120 or below. Families between 200% and 400% FPL may still qualify for discounted care, not full write-off, so it's worth screening everyone who has received a hospital bill — even if they think they earn too much. Reassure families that this is not charity in the stigmatized sense; it is a legal entitlement they paid into through the tax-exempt status of the hospital.

**Q3: A neighbor says, "I already paid my hospital bill — it's too late for charity care." Is that true?**
**A (Short):** Not necessarily — families can apply for charity care retroactively in many cases, even after a bill has been paid or sent to collections.
**A (Full):** Illinois law and most hospital charity care policies allow retroactive applications, sometimes up to 240 days after the date of service. Even if a family already paid, they may be eligible for a refund if they qualify. If the bill has gone to a collection agency, it can be recalled or disputed once a charity care determination is made. The CEN team should open a case right away and let the Charity Care Navigator workflow evaluate the timeline — do not assume it is too late without running the screening.

**Q4: What myths or fears stop families from applying for charity care, and how do I address them?**
**A (Short):** Common fears include stigma, immigration status concerns, and worry that applying will hurt their credit — none of these should prevent a family from applying.
**A (Full):** Many community members fear that asking for charity marks them as a burden, or that it will somehow appear on their record. In fact, charity care applications are confidential and do not appear on credit reports. For mixed-status or undocumented families, it is important to know that charity care applications at hospitals do not trigger immigration enforcement and hospitals are not required to verify citizenship for charity care eligibility. If a family owes money and that bill goes to collections while an application is pending, a good-faith charity care application can often pause collection activity. FairClaims trains CEN teams to address these concerns with empathy and accurate information.

**A (Short):** FairClaims provides end-to-end advocacy — screening, paperwork completion, follow-up, and appeal support — that a family calling billing on their own rarely receives.

**Q7: What information do I need to gather from the family before their first session?**
**A (Short):** Before the first session, collect the family's household size, recent income documentation, the hospital bill or Explanation of Benefits (EOB), and any prior correspondence with the hospital's billing department.
**A (Full):** The Charity Care Navigator workflow requires: (1) proof of household income — pay stubs from the last 30 days, most recent tax return, or a self-attestation form if no documentation is available; (2) a copy of the hospital bill or collection notice with the account number; (3) household size, including all dependents; (4) the name of the hospital or health system (needed to look up their specific charity care policy); and (5) any denial letters if the family already applied. If the family has none of these, that's okay — the Discovery stage workflow includes nodes that help generate self-attestation letters and request itemized bills from the hospital. Don't let missing documents block you from opening the case.

**Q8: How does the CEN platform determine whether a family qualifies for charity care?**
**A (Short):** The Charity Care Navigator runs a structured income-to-FPL calculation at a CONDITION node and branches the workflow based on the result — free care, discounted care, or additional screening needed.
**A (Full):** After intake, the workflow reaches a CONDITION node (shown in orange in the DAG Viewer) that compares household income to the current FPL grid. If income is at or below 200% FPL, the workflow branches to the full charity care application path. If income is between 200% and 400% FPL, it branches to the discounted care path. If income appears above threshold, the workflow checks for extraordinary medical expenses, recent job loss, or other hardship factors before concluding that the family is out of scope. The platform does not make the final eligibility decision — that is made by the hospital — but it ensures the strongest possible application is submitted. I can't give you a definitive eligibility ruling, but the workflow will walk you through every factor.

**Q9: The family doesn't have pay stubs — what do I do during the intake screen?**
**A (Short):** Use the self-attestation path in the workflow, which generates a signed income declaration that most Illinois hospitals must accept under their charity care policies.
**A (Full):** Illinois nonprofit hospitals are required to have a financial assistance application process that accommodates applicants who cannot provide traditional income documentation. The Charity Care Navigator includes a self-attestation branch — when you indicate "No documentation available" at the income verification node, the platform generates a pre-formatted Income Self-Declaration letter that the family can sign. Upload the signed letter as an artifact in the case. Some hospitals will ask for a follow-up affidavit; the workflow will flag that as a pending step if needed. Self-attestation is a legitimate and recognized path — do not stall the case waiting for documents that may never materialize.

**Q11: What does the Charity Care Navigator workflow actually look like once I start it — what are the main stages?**
**A (Short):** The workflow moves through income screening, hospital policy lookup, application preparation, document collection, application submission, and then a decision-tracking phase with an appeal branch if needed.
**A (Full):** The Charity Care Navigator DAG has 34 nodes organized into roughly six phases: (1) intake and income pre-screen (CONDITION node), (2) hospital policy lookup and FAP identification (ACTION nodes), (3) application packet assembly — completing the FAP, gathering supporting documents, and attaching artifacts, (4) submission — either uploading directly to the hospital portal or generating a physical packet, (5) a tracking phase where the workflow monitors the 30-day hospital response window, and (6) a decision branch — approval (close case), partial approval (accept or appeal), or denial (trigger the appeal sub-path). The DAG Viewer in the platform lets you see exactly where the case sits at any moment. Each node tells you what action is needed and what comes next.

**Q12: What is the navigator's role versus what the platform does automatically in the Charity Care workflow?**
**A (Short):** The platform structures the process, generates documents, and tracks timelines; the navigator builds trust with the family, gathers information, makes judgment calls at CONDITION nodes, and advocates with the hospital.
**A (Full):** The CEN platform is a workflow engine, not an autonomous agent. At each ACTION node, the platform tells the navigator what task needs to be completed (e.g., "Upload signed income declaration"), but the human navigator actually gathers that document from the family and uploads it. At CONDITION nodes, the navigator reviews the branching options and selects the correct path based on real information from the family. The platform automates document generation, pre-fills known data into the FAP, tracks deadlines, and sends reminders. The navigator's irreplaceable value is in the relationship — families often share information with a trusted community member that they would never disclose to a hospital staffer. That trust is what makes the workflow function correctly.

**Q13: How long does the charity care application process typically take from start to finish?**
**A (Short):** A complete application can typically be assembled in one to two sessions; hospitals then have up to 30 days to respond, and appeals can add another 30–60 days.
**A (Full):** For a family with clear documentation, the navigator can complete the entire application packet in a single 45–60 minute session. If documents need to be gathered (pay stubs, tax returns, self-attestation letters), a second session is usually needed. Once submitted, Illinois hospitals are generally expected to respond within 30 days, though policies vary by institution. If the response is a denial or partial approval that requires appeal, add 30–60 days. The platform's case timeline view shows you the elapsed time at each stage and flags any step that has gone past its expected window. If a hospital is unresponsive, the workflow includes an escalation node to contact the hospital's financial assistance office directly or involve the Illinois Attorney General's Healthcare Advocate.

**Q14: What happens at the CONDITION node that decides "free care" vs. "discounted care" — can I override it?**
**A (Short):** The CONDITION node calculates the FPL percentage from the income and household size you entered; you can update those inputs if you entered them incorrectly, but you cannot bypass the logic.
**A (Full):** The FPL CONDITION node uses the values you entered during intake to run the calculation. If the result seems wrong — for example, if you entered monthly income instead of annual — you can go back to the intake record, correct the value, and re-evaluate the node. The platform does not allow you to manually force a different branch without correcting the underlying data, which protects the integrity of the case record. If there are unusual circumstances (a one-time income spike, a recent layoff), there is a "Hardship Override" input field at the CONDITION node that lets you document the reason and flag the case for navigator supervisor review. All overrides are logged in the audit trail.

**Q15: What does the "Rewind" feature do, and when should I use it in the Charity Care workflow?**
**A (Short):** Rewind rolls the case back to a previous node without deleting data, letting you correct a mistake or re-evaluate a branch with new information.
**A (Full):** Rewind is accessible from the DAG Viewer by right-clicking any completed node and selecting "Rewind to Here." The platform will move the case's active position back to that node while preserving all previously entered data as a recoverable version. For example, if you submitted the wrong income figure and already passed the FPL CONDITION node, you can Rewind to the income entry node, correct the value, and re-run the condition. You should use Rewind sparingly and document the reason for rewinding in the case notes field, because it creates an audit trail entry. Do not use Rewind to skip steps — it is for correction, not shortcutting. Supervisors can review all Rewind events in the case audit log.

**Q17: What is an APPROVAL node, and what does it mean when the Charity Care case hits one?**
**A (Short):** An APPROVAL node means the workflow has reached a step that requires a human sign-off — usually a supervisor review of a hardship override, a large potential relief amount, or a ready-to-submit application packet.
**A (Full):** APPROVAL nodes (shown in purple in the DAG Viewer) pause the workflow until a designated reviewer — typically a FairClaims case supervisor — logs in and approves the step. In the Charity Care Navigator, you'll encounter APPROVAL nodes before submitting an application that involves a hardship override or when the estimated relief value exceeds a certain threshold (set by your program). The assigned reviewer receives an email notification and can review the full case record and attached artifacts before approving or sending it back with notes. As a navigator, you cannot advance past an APPROVAL node on your own — this is by design to ensure quality control. You can send a reminder to the reviewer from the node's action menu if approval is taking too long.

**Q81: What is a prior authorization, and why does it matter to families we serve?**
**A (Short):** Prior authorization is a requirement by health insurance plans that certain medications, procedures, or services be approved before they are provided — and denials of these requests frequently block families from care they need.

**Q84: What are the most common reasons insurance companies give for denying a prior authorization?**
**A (Short):** The most common denial reasons are "not medically necessary," "experimental/investigational," "step therapy required" (try a cheaper drug first), or administrative errors like missing information in the request.
**A (Full):** Prior auth denials typically fall into a few categories: (1) "Not medically necessary" — the insurer's internal criteria don't match the doctor's clinical judgment; this is the most common and most frequently overturned on appeal. (2) "Experimental or investigational" — the treatment isn't on the insurer's approved list even if it's standard of care elsewhere. (3) "Step therapy" — the plan requires the patient to try and fail a less expensive alternative first; Illinois has laws limiting how step therapy can be applied. (4) "Non-covered service" — the plan simply doesn't cover this type of service; this is harder to appeal on medical grounds but may have alternative solutions. (5) Administrative — the doctor's office submitted incomplete information; these are often the easiest to fix. Understanding which category a denial falls into determines the appeal strategy.

**Q85: What is an external appeal, and when does FairClaims help families use it?**
**A (Short):** An external appeal is a review by an independent organization — not the insurance company — that the insurer is legally required to accept; FairClaims helps families request one when internal appeals are exhausted or taking too long.
**A (Full):** After a family exhausts their internal appeals (typically one or two levels within the insurance company), they have the right to request an Independent Medical Review (also called an external appeal) by a neutral third-party reviewer. In Illinois, the Department of Insurance oversees this process. The external reviewer — who has no financial relationship with the insurer — looks at the medical evidence and makes a binding determination that the insurer must follow. This process costs nothing for the patient and must be completed within defined timelines (often 60 days for standard reviews, much faster for urgent cases). FairClaims helps families request external appeals when internal appeals have failed, when the insurer is not responding within required timelines, or when the case is urgent enough to request an expedited review. This is one of the most powerful tools available — and almost no one knows it exists.

**Q94: What is a "peer-to-peer review" and should the family's doctor request one?**

**Q122: What is workers' compensation, and do all workers in Illinois qualify?**

**Q123: What can FairClaims help with beyond the standard workers' comp claim — are there other legal avenues?**

**Q130: What information do I need to calculate the worker's average weekly wage for benefit purposes?**
**A (Short):** Collect 52 weeks of pay stubs or earnings records (W-2 or 1099), including overtime, tips, and any other regular compensation — the Illinois Workers' Compensation Commission uses a specific formula for this calculation.

**Q135: What is the difference between a "temporary disability" benefit and a "permanent disability" award?**
**A (Short):** Temporary disability benefits replace wages while the worker can't work and end when they return to work or reach maximum medical improvement; permanent disability awards compensate for lasting impairment and are calculated differently.
**A (Full):** In Illinois workers' comp, temporary total disability (TTD) benefits are two-thirds of the worker's average weekly wage, paid while the worker is unable to work during treatment and recovery. These benefits stop when the worker returns to work or when their treating physician says they have reached "maximum medical improvement" (MMI) — the point where the condition is as good as it's going to get. At MMI, if the worker has a lasting impairment, they may be entitled to a permanent partial disability (PPD) award — a one-time payment based on the impairment rating (percentage of loss of use of a body part) and the body part value set by Illinois statute. If the injury is so severe the worker can never return to any employment, they may qualify for permanent total disability (PTD) — ongoing benefits at two-thirds AWW potentially for life. Temporary partial disability (TPD) applies when the worker returns to lighter duty at a reduced wage — they receive two-thirds of the difference between pre-injury and post-injury earnings. I can explain the general process, but this is not legal advice — specific guidance should come from an attorney.

**Q143: What should I do if the employer's workers' comp insurer sends the worker to an "Independent Medical Examination" (IME)?**

**Q162: How do I explain a "mass tort" or "class action" to a family who has never heard these terms?**
**A (Short):** A mass tort is when many people harmed by the same product, chemical, or company come together in coordinated litigation — each person's case remains individual, but the cases are managed together for efficiency and leverage.
**A (Full):** Start with the basic concept: if a company's product or pollution hurts hundreds or thousands of people, it makes no sense for each person to sue separately. Instead, lawyers bring coordinated cases where the common facts (the product, the chemical, the company's conduct) are proven once, for everyone. In a mass tort, each plaintiff still has their own case and their own damages — unlike a class action, where a single judgment covers the whole group. Mass torts are especially common in toxic exposure cases, where a product (like asbestos insulation or talcum powder) or an industrial chemical (like PFAS) has harmed many people in similar ways. The Judicial Panel on Multidistrict Litigation (JPML) consolidates these cases in a single federal court for efficient management. Once common issues are resolved, individual cases may settle separately. FairClaims helps families understand whether they may qualify for an active mass tort or class action program, and connects them with attorneys who handle these cases on contingency — meaning no upfront cost to the family.

**Q164: What is the timeline situation with toxic exposure claims — are there deadlines families need to know about?**
**A (Short):** Yes — toxic exposure claims are subject to statutes of limitations that vary by claim type and state, and some settlement programs have enrollment deadlines; acting promptly is important even if the exposure happened years ago.

PFAS and hair relaxer MDLs are actively accepting claims now but litigation timelines evolve. The key message: even if the exposure happened 10 or 20 years ago, there may still be a viable claim — but waiting makes it less likely. **Q197: What do I do if the attorney sends back the case saying the client doesn't qualify — how do I handle that with the family?**
**A (Short):** Update the case with the attorney's determination, explain clearly to the family what the attorney found and why, set a monitoring flag for new programs, and offer any applicable alternative support resources.
**A (Full):** Attorney declines happen, and the family deserves an honest, respectful explanation. When the attorney communicates their determination, log it in the attorney contact log with the specific reason (ineligible diagnosis, insufficient exposure documentation, claim is time-barred, etc.). Then schedule a conversation with the family to explain: (1) What the attorney found and why the case doesn't meet current eligibility criteria. (2) Whether there are other programs that might apply (re-run the eligibility screening). (3) Whether additional documentation might change the outcome (if the claim was declined for insufficient documentation, explain what would be needed). (4) What the monitoring options are — the case will remain in Passive Monitoring and the family will be notified if new programs emerge. At the "Attorney Decline" outcome node, select the reason from the dropdown and add free-text notes. The platform will automatically set a monitoring flag and include the case in future program-match alerts. Also offer: community health partner referrals for ongoing medical care; SDOH resource referrals if there are other unmet needs; and connection to the Illinois EPA or ATSDR for environmental health information about their neighborhood.

**Q200: If I'm new to the CEN platform and the toxic exposure module — what is the single most important thing to know when handling these cases?**
**A (Short):** The most important thing is to document everything thoroughly and promptly — the exposure timeline, health conditions, and environmental evidence you gather in the early sessions may become critical legal evidence years down the line, even for cases that seem inconclusive today.
**A (Full):** Toxic exposure cases are unique among FairClaims' five pillars because the connection between what a community member experienced years or decades ago and their current health may not be proven until long after the CEN session. A thorough, carefully documented case record created today may be the difference between a family receiving compensation and losing their claim to a statute of limitations or insufficient evidence. Key principles for new navigators: (1) Document what you know AND what you don't know — uncertainty is normal and should be recorded, not glossed over. (2) Every address, every product, every employer matters — the details that seem minor today may be legally significant later. (3) These cases take years — set appropriate expectations with families and set monitoring reminders so you don't lose track. (4) You are not a lawyer — your job is to gather, document, screen, and connect. The attorney evaluates legal sufficiency. (5) Community patterns matter — if you see multiple families from the same neighborhood with similar exposures, document the cluster and flag it for advocacy. (6) The AI Concierge is your partner — use it at every node, especially when you're unsure what question to ask next. Toxic exposure work is among the most impactful work a community health navigator can do. A single case, carefully documented, can connect a family to justice for harm they experienced years ago — and contribute to accountability for the systems that caused the harm.
